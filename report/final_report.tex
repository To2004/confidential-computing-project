\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Homomorphic encryption lets a server compute on data it cannot read. It closes the
one gap that ordinary encryption leaves open: data is protected at rest and in
transit, but not while it is being used. The price is speed, and this project
measures how high that price is. We implement the same operations three times ---
in plain Python, in TenSEAL, and in OpenFHE --- and measure runtime, memory,
ciphertext size and accuracy. Every figure is a mean over 1000 repetitions after a
discarded warm-up. Our main finding is that there is no single overhead number. In
TenSEAL, encrypted addition costs $153\times$ a plaintext addition, but summing one
encrypted vector costs $32{,}832\times$ its plaintext equivalent. What separates
them is whether the operation has to move data between ciphertext slots. Re-running
OpenFHE at TenSEAL's parameters then cuts its multiplication penalty from
$6.89\times$ to $3.16\times$, so much of the gap between the libraries is really a
gap between configurations. Applied to a synthetic healthcare score over 1000
records, the encrypted computation finishes in 214\,ms, matches the plaintext answer
to five significant figures, and costs $2{,}865\times$ plaintext rather than the
$13{,}450\times$ measured on isolated operations --- because a full ciphertext
amortises a cost that CKKS charges per ciphertext, not per value.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Encryption protects data at rest and in transit, but not while it is being used. To
compute on encrypted data, a normal system has to decrypt it first, and at that
moment the plaintext is exposed to whatever machine is doing the work. Homomorphic
encryption (HE) removes that step: operations on ciphertexts produce ciphertexts
that decrypt to the correct answer, so a cloud provider can process data it never
sees.

The idea is old, but usable libraries are recent. That turns a theoretical
capability into a practical decision: if a hospital or a bank wanted to outsource a
computation over sensitive records today, what would it cost, and which library
should it choose? Answering that needs measurement. Published complexity results say
little about what a particular library does with a particular operation on a
particular machine.

This project measures it. We compare two CKKS libraries, \textbf{TenSEAL} and
\textbf{OpenFHE}, against an unencrypted baseline over a small set of operations
common in data analysis, and report runtime, memory, ciphertext size, accuracy, and
the practical problems we met while building them.

\paragraph{Research question.} \emph{What is the practical overhead of performing
basic computations using two different homomorphic encryption implementations,
compared to equivalent plaintext computation?}

The report follows the measurements in the order they led to one another.
Section~\ref{sec:background} explains the scheme and the libraries,
Section~\ref{sec:impl} what we built and the three experiments we ran, and
Section~\ref{sec:setup} how we measured them. Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the
core result: what each operation costs. That result raises an immediate question ---
are we measuring the libraries, or the settings we gave them? ---
which Section~\ref{sec:params} answers. Section~\ref{sec:usecase} then puts
everything together in a healthcare example, and
Sections~\ref{sec:usability}--\ref{sec:limits} cover usability and limitations.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Homomorphic encryption and CKKS}

Both libraries implement \textbf{CKKS}~\cite{ckks2017}, a scheme for approximate
arithmetic on real numbers. Approximate is the important word: decrypting a CKKS
result gives the correct value plus a small error, in exchange for much better
performance than exact schemes on real-valued data. For statistics over measurements
that trade is usually fine, and Section~\ref{sec:accuracy} shows the error we
actually saw.

Four properties of CKKS shape every measurement in this report.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ring dimension.} Ciphertexts are polynomials. The degree of that
polynomial --- the ring dimension --- sets the security level and drives the cost of
every operation. A bigger ring is more secure and uniformly slower.
\item \textbf{Slot packing.} One ciphertext holds many values in parallel
``slots'', and one operation acts on all of them at once. The cost depends on the
ring dimension, not on how many slots hold real data.
\item \textbf{Multiplicative depth.} Each multiplication uses up one level from a
budget fixed at setup. Running past the budget does not slow the computation down;
it makes the answer wrong.
\item \textbf{Rotations.} Slots cannot be read individually. Summing across them
means rotating the ciphertext and adding, about $\log_2(\text{slots})$ times, and
every rotation needs an expensive key switch. This is why summation costs so much
more than addition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The two libraries}

\textbf{TenSEAL} is a Python library built on Microsoft SEAL. It presents encrypted
vectors as ordinary Python objects that support \texttt{+}, \texttt{*} and
\texttt{.sum()}, and handles relinearization and rescaling internally. The
programmer gives it the modulus chain directly.

\textbf{OpenFHE} is a C++ library with Python bindings, maintained by an academic
consortium. It exposes lower-level calls (\texttt{EvalAdd}, \texttt{EvalMult},
\texttt{EvalSum}) and needs evaluation keys to be generated explicitly. The
programmer gives it a multiplicative depth and a scaling size, and the library works
out the ring dimension that satisfies them at the requested security level.

That difference matters more than it looks. Given what we considered comparable
settings, the two libraries ended up at different ring dimensions --- 8192 for
TenSEAL and 16384 for OpenFHE --- which we return to in Section~\ref{sec:params}.

\subsection{Related work}

Comparing HE libraries is not new. Sathya et al.~\cite{sathya2018} survey the
available libraries and their trade-offs. Faneela et al.~\cite{faneela2025}
benchmark SEAL against OpenFHE across BGV and CKKS on two operating systems and
conclude that OpenFHE performs better. Takeshita et al.~\cite{heprofiler2025}
profile four CKKS implementations and discuss directly how hard it is to compare
libraries whose architectures differ. Viand et al.~\cite{viand2021} survey the
compiler layer above these libraries.

Our ranking differs from Faneela et al., which is one reason
Section~\ref{sec:params} exists: we found the ranking depends on the parameters each
library is given. We claim no new method over the work above. This is a course
project, and its goal is a clear practical measurement of two libraries we
implemented ourselves.

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:impl}

We implemented the same seven operations three times: in plain Python as a baseline,
in TenSEAL, and in OpenFHE.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Operation} & \textbf{What it does} \\
\midrule
Key generation & Build the crypto context and generate the keys the library needs \\
Encryption & Encode a vector of numbers into a ciphertext \\
Addition & Element-wise sum of two encrypted vectors \\
Multiplication & Element-wise product of two encrypted vectors \\
Summation & Sum of all elements inside one encrypted vector \\
Average & Summation, then multiplication by $1/n$ \\
Dot product & Element-wise multiplication, then summation \\
Decryption & Recover the plaintext result \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The implemented operations. All except key generation and encryption have a
direct plaintext counterpart, which is the baseline.}
\end{table}

The two libraries need different code for the same computation. Encrypted addition
in TenSEAL is \texttt{enc + enc}; in OpenFHE it is \texttt{cc.EvalAdd(ct, ct)}.
Summation is \texttt{enc.sum()} against \texttt{cc.EvalSum(ct, n)}, and the OpenFHE
version needs summation keys generated beforehand.

One incompatibility showed up immediately and affected everything after it:
\textbf{OpenFHE requires the batch size to be a power of two}, while TenSEAL accepts
any length. Every input has to be padded up to the next power of two. For simple
operations that is harmless, but Section~\ref{sec:usecase} shows a case where the
padding \emph{value} has to be chosen carefully.

Each operation is timed on freshly encrypted inputs rather than on the output of the
previous repetition, so no measurement inherits accumulated noise or a consumed
level. All three implementations share one measurement harness, so the plaintext,
TenSEAL and OpenFHE columns are produced by the same timing and memory code and
differ only in the operation being called.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{What we measure, and how}

\textbf{Runtime.} Each operation is timed on its own with
\texttt{time.perf\_counter} around a single call. Encryption and decryption are
timed separately from the operations between them, so the cost of getting data in
and out is never folded into the cost of computing on it.

\textbf{Repetitions and warm-up.} One timing of an HE operation is not
reproducible. Every figure in this report is the \textbf{mean of 1000 timed
repetitions} after \textbf{50 warm-up calls that are discarded}. The warm-up matters
because the first calls into either library pay costs that are not part of normal
operation: loading the native library, building lookup tables on first use, and
faulting in fresh memory. Counting them would inflate the mean and make it depend on
how many repetitions were run. Garbage collection is off inside the timed loop, so
an unrelated collection is not charged to the operation. Every repetition starts
from the same freshly encrypted inputs, so no repetition inherits noise or a
consumed level from the one before it.

\textbf{Memory.} We report two figures, because one is not enough. Python's
\texttt{tracemalloc} sees only Python-level allocations, and both libraries keep
ciphertext coefficients in native C++ memory that Python never learns about. So we
also allocate many ciphertexts, hold them, and measure how much the process resident
set grows. Memory is measured in a separate pass from timing, because
\texttt{tracemalloc} hooks every allocation and distorts short measurements.

\textbf{Ciphertext size.} Each library's own serialization routine, in bytes,
compared against the plaintext vector stored as 8-byte doubles.

\textbf{Accuracy.} Every encrypted result is compared against the plaintext
computation of the same values, and we report the largest absolute error. Each
benchmark checks this error against a loose bound before reporting any timing, so a
run with wrong parameters fails visibly instead of producing plausible but
meaningless numbers.

\subsection{Machine and configuration}

Measurements ran on a compute node reserved exclusively for the job, so nothing else
shared the machine. We fixed the thread count to one
(\texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=1}): both libraries use multithreaded native code, and on
a shared machine their measured cost would depend on how many cores each happened to
get, which is not a property of the library.

Absolute timings depend on this hardware, so the \emph{ratios} between the three

Three things stand out.

\textbf{There is no single overhead figure.} Inside TenSEAL alone the cost runs from
$153\times$ for addition to $32{,}832\times$ for summation. A claim like ``HE is ten
thousand times slower'' is therefore not usable: the answer depends on which
operation dominates the workload.

\textbf{Addition is nearly free; operations that need rotations are not.} Encrypted
addition takes 96 microseconds, a fiftieth of a multiplication and a hundredth of a
summation. The reason is structural. Addition works on ciphertext coefficients
directly. Summation, average and dot product have to move data between slots, and
every rotation carries a key switch. This one mechanism explains the order of every
row in the table, and it is the most useful thing we measured: a computation built
from additions and constant multiplications will be far cheaper than one that has to
aggregate across slots.

\textbf{TenSEAL is faster here, on every operation.} It leads by $2.75\times$ on
encryption and $6.85\times$ on multiplication. Section~\ref{sec:params} tests whether
that reflects the libraries or the parameters we gave them.

\subsection{Key generation}

Key generation happens once per session rather than once per operation, so we timed
it separately, over 50 repetitions.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{TenSEAL} & \textbf{OpenFHE} \\
\midrule
Context and key generation & 216.180\,ms & 208.308\,ms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

This is the one measurement where OpenFHE leads, and the two are within 4\% of each
other. The more useful observation is the scale: TenSEAL spends 216\,ms building
keys and 38\,ms doing all five encrypted operations. Setup costs almost six times
the computation, so a service that builds a fresh context per request would spend
most of its time generating keys rather than doing the work it was asked to do.

The first row is why we measured memory two ways. On its own, \texttt{tracemalloc}
suggests a ciphertext costs under a kilobyte, which is wrong by a factor of about
600: it is measuring the Python wrapper, not the encrypted data behind it. The
resident-set figure and the serialized size agree with each other to within 7\%,
which is reassuring, since they are independent measurements of the same object.

The practical consequence is the last row. Five numbers take 40 bytes in the clear
and 334\,KB encrypted under TenSEAL --- an expansion of about $8{,}400\times$. For a
cloud deployment this, not RAM, is the binding constraint, because it is what has to
cross the network. Section~\ref{sec:usecase} shows the ratio improving sharply once
the ciphertext slots are actually used.

We also measured the evaluation keys, which the server needs before it can compute
at all. At the configuration used in Section~\ref{sec:usecase} they are
\textbf{179.7\,MB} for TenSEAL and \textbf{69.2\,MB} for OpenFHE, against roughly
5\,MB of encrypted data. They are sent once per context rather than per query, but
they are by far the largest object involved.

larger scaling factor, $2^{50}$ against TenSEAL's $2^{40}$, which keeps about ten
more bits of every value. Section~\ref{sec:params} shows that is not the whole story.

\section{The Libraries, or the Parameters?}\label{sec:params}

Section~\ref{sec:runtime} found TenSEAL faster on every operation and
Section~\ref{sec:accuracy} found OpenFHE more precise. Both comparisons share a
weakness: the two libraries were not running equivalent parameters. OpenFHE had
chosen ring dimension 16384 against TenSEAL's 8192, so it was doing roughly twice the
polynomial work per operation, and it was using a $2^{50}$ scaling factor against
$2^{40}$, so it was keeping ten more bits of every value. We cannot credit either
result to the library until those differences are removed.

OpenFHE lets the ring dimension be set explicitly, so we re-ran it pinned to 8192
with a $2^{40}$ scale, matching TenSEAL. We also varied OpenFHE's \emph{scaling
technique}, an internal option controlling how it manages the scaling factor between
multiplications, which TenSEAL does not expose at all.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{OpenFHE configuration} & \textbf{Encr} & \textbf{Add} & \textbf{Mult} &
\textbf{Sum} & \textbf{Dot} & \textbf{Decr} & \textbf{Geo. mean} \\
\midrule
Own defaults (ring 16384, $2^{50}$)          & 2.52 & 4.02 & 6.89 & 7.24 & 8.70 & 16.28 & \textbf{6.44} \\
Matched, same scaling technique              & 1.20 & 2.12 & 3.16 & 3.25 & 3.97 & 7.83  & \textbf{3.05} \\
Matched, fastest scaling technique           & 1.02 & 1.77 & 1.93 & 2.41 & 3.09 & 6.80  & \textbf{2.37} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{OpenFHE runtime as a multiple of TenSEAL's, per operation. Lower is closer
to TenSEAL.}
\end{table}

Matching the parameters closes most of the gap. Multiplication drops from
$6.89\times$ to $3.16\times$, encryption becomes almost identical at $1.20\times$,
and across all six operations the geometric mean falls from $6.44\times$ to
$3.05\times$. Separating the two changes for multiplication, the ring dimension
accounts for a factor of 2.18 and the scaling technique for a further 1.64.

Two qualifications go with this result. Decryption is still $7.8\times$ slower even
matched, so the gap narrows everywhere but closes nowhere. And pinning the ring
dimension overrides OpenFHE's own security check: asked to pick a ring for these
parameters at 128-bit security, OpenFHE chooses 16384 and refuses 8192. The matched
configuration therefore buys comparability by giving up security, which is fine for
an experiment isolating one variable but would not be acceptable in deployment.

What we take from this is that \textbf{a benchmark of two HE libraries at their
default settings measures the defaults as much as the libraries}. It also suggests
why Faneela et al.~\cite{faneela2025} rank OpenFHE above SEAL where we rank TenSEAL
above OpenFHE: at default settings, the two are not doing equivalent work.

\section{Use Case: Privacy-Preserving Healthcare Analytics}\label{sec:usecase}

Everything so far concerns isolated operations. A real workload combines them, and
combining them raises problems that single operations do not. To see that, we
implemented the motivating scenario from our proposal: a hospital wants a cloud
provider to compute a score for every patient and the average over the cohort,
without exposing any individual record.

\paragraph{The score.} We define a \textbf{synthetic risk score} over five
measurements per patient. \emph{This score is built for demonstration only. It is not
a medical model and has no clinical meaning.} Each feature is first normalised to
roughly $[0,1]$ against a fixed public range,
$x_{\text{norm}} = (x - \min)/(\max - \min)$, using age 18--90, systolic blood
pressure 90--180, BMI 18--40, cholesterol 120--280 and glucose 70--200. The score is
then
\begin{align*}
\text{score} = 100 \cdot \big(\;
& 0.15\,a + 0.20\,b + 0.15\,m + 0.15\,c + 0.15\,g \\
+\; & 0.10\,b\,g + 0.05\,m\,c + 0.05\,b^{2} \;\big),
\end{align*}
where $a,b,m,c,g$ are the normalised age, blood pressure, BMI, cholesterol and
glucose. The eight weights sum to 1, so a patient at the top of every range scores
100.

The shape was chosen to stress the library, not to model medicine. The first five
terms are a weighted average and need only multiplication by public constants. The
last three are what make it a useful test: two interaction terms and one square, each
multiplying one ciphertext by another. That is the operation that consumes
multiplicative depth, and it is why this computation cannot run under the parameters
of Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\paragraph{The data.} A synthetic cohort of 1000 patients, generated from a fixed
seed. Age is drawn uniformly across its range; the four clinical measures are drawn
from normal distributions centred on plausible values and then clipped to the ranges
above, which keeps every normalised value inside $[0,1]$. The resulting scores have
mean 35.63 and standard deviation 8.45.

\paragraph{Encrypting it.} We encrypt one \emph{feature} per ciphertext, one patient
per slot, giving five ciphertexts of 1000 values each. A single homomorphic
multiplication then advances all 1000 patients at once. The alternative --- one
ciphertext per patient --- would multiply the number of operations by a thousand.

The padding rule from Section~\ref{sec:impl} gets delicate here. Vectors are padded
from 1000 to 1024 slots, and we fill the padding with \emph{each feature's minimum},
not with zero. A minimum normalises to exactly 0, so padded slots contribute nothing
and drop out of the cohort sum. Zero-padding would instead give those slots a
normalised value of $-\min/(\max-\min)$, which is not zero, and would quietly corrupt
the average. That is exactly the kind of bug that produces a plausible wrong answer,
so we cover it with a unit test.

\paragraph{Depth.} Normalisation, the interaction terms, the weights and the cohort
mean use four levels in total. Neither library can run that at the
Section~\ref{sec:results} settings: TenSEAL fails with \texttt{scale out of bounds},
and a longer modulus chain does not fit at ring 8192, so the ring has to double to
16384. OpenFHE evaluates the score at depth 3 but fails on the cohort mean and needs
depth 4. Adding two interaction terms and a square to a weighted average was enough
to force new cryptographic parameters and double the ring dimension --- in CKKS, the
shape of a computation matters more than the amount of data in it.

The whole scenario takes \textbf{214\,ms} with TenSEAL and 411\,ms with OpenFHE, of
which the cloud does 135\,ms and 309\,ms. Per patient that is 0.21 and 0.41\,ms, and
the encrypted cohort mean matches the plaintext value to five significant figures.

The overhead is worth comparing against Section~\ref{sec:results}: $2{,}865\times$
here against $13{,}450\times$ for the isolated operations. Nothing got faster. The
ciphertexts got fuller --- 1024 slots carrying real data instead of 5 --- and a CKKS
operation costs what the ring dimension says it costs, however many slots actually
hold data. Measuring HE on toy inputs therefore overstates its cost.

The same effect shows up in data volume. Each encrypted feature is about 1\,MB, so
the hospital uploads roughly 5\,MB for a cohort that takes 40\,KB in the clear --- an
expansion of $132\times$, against the $8{,}400\times$ measured on the 5-element
vector. The evaluation keys, at 179.7\,MB, are still larger than the data.

Two caveats. Encryption and decryption are done by the hospital, so 79\,ms of the
214\,ms is work the client does rather than work it avoids; for a computation this
small, outsourcing would not pay for itself, and the scenario is better read as a
stand-in for workloads where it would. And our benchmark runs client and server in
one process, so it measures the computation faithfully but not the network transfer
or key management a real deployment would also need.

TenSEAL was clearly easier to work with. Encrypted code looks like the plaintext code
next to it, and the context handles keys without being asked. OpenFHE asks more of
the programmer: evaluation keys generated explicitly, batch sizes rounded to powers
of two, and several concepts named that TenSEAL keeps internal.

That verbosity is not only a cost. OpenFHE makes you declare the multiplicative depth
up front, so exceeding it fails immediately and says so. TenSEAL finds the same
problem later and reports \texttt{scale out of bounds}, whose connection to the
length of the modulus chain is not obvious to a newcomer --- we spent noticeably
longer diagnosing that failure in TenSEAL than the equivalent in OpenFHE. OpenFHE
also exposes controls, such as the scaling technique in Section~\ref{sec:params},
that turn out to matter and that TenSEAL does not offer at all.

\section{Practical Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Met during development.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{OpenFHE installs only on Linux, and only for one Python version.} Its
wheel ships a binary built for CPython 3.8; installing it under 3.9 or later appears
to work and then fails at import. This fixed the Python version for the whole
project.
\item \textbf{Power-of-two batch sizes.} Every input has to be padded, and
Section~\ref{sec:usecase} shows the padding value is not always free to choose.
\item \textbf{Multiplicative depth has to be planned in advance.} Adding two
interaction terms to a weighted average forced new parameters and doubled the ring
dimension. Asking for more depth than needed costs just as much: our first attempt
requested depth 5, which works but pushes the ring to 32768 and slows every operation
for no benefit.
\item \textbf{Measuring memory is not straightforward.} The obvious tool reports a
figure about 600 times too small, because the data lives outside Python.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations of this study.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Measurements come from one machine, one CPU generation and a single thread. The
ratios between columns transfer better than the absolute times.
\item The plaintext baseline is ordinary Python. A NumPy or C baseline would be
faster, so our overhead figures are upper bounds relative to optimised plaintext.
\item Each configuration was measured in one process run, so variation between
processes is not captured.
\item Neither library was tested with bootstrapping, so all results concern
computations that fit inside a fixed level budget.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

The research question asked what basic computations cost under homomorphic
encryption compared with plaintext. The measurements give a specific answer and,
more usefully, explain what drives it.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{The overhead is a range, not a number.} Encrypted operations cost
between $153\times$ and $32{,}832\times$ their plaintext equivalents in TenSEAL, and
between $615\times$ and $237{,}943\times$ in OpenFHE. Any single quoted figure hides a
factor of two hundred.

\item \textbf{What an operation costs depends on whether it needs rotations.}
Addition and constant multiplication work on ciphertexts directly and are cheap.
Summation, average and dot product have to move data between slots, and every
rotation carries a key switch. Structuring a computation to avoid aggregation across
slots is the single most effective optimisation available.

\item \textbf{Much of the difference between the libraries was a difference in
parameters.} Re-running OpenFHE at TenSEAL's ring dimension and scale cut its
multiplication penalty from $6.89\times$ to $3.16\times$ and made encryption nearly
identical. Comparing two HE libraries at their defaults is partly a comparison of the
defaults.

\item \textbf{Real computations are cheaper than isolated operations suggest, so
ciphertexts should be filled.} The healthcare scenario --- 1000 records, interaction
terms, a cohort aggregate --- ran at $2{,}865\times$ plaintext, a fifth of the
isolated benchmark's overhead, and agreed with the plaintext result to five
significant figures. Nothing became faster; the ciphertexts simply carried 1024
values instead of 5, and CKKS charges per ciphertext rather than per value. The
largest data cost was not the ciphertexts but the evaluation keys: 179.7\,MB against
5\,MB of encrypted data.
\end{enumerate}

Homomorphic encryption is usable today for the kind of computation examined here, as
long as the workload is batched, structured to avoid unnecessary rotations, and given
parameters chosen deliberately rather than by default. The cost is real, but it is
dominated by choices the programmer controls.

Two further experiments are included in the project repository rather than here: a
sweep of the pipeline over input sizes from 5 to 500, which measures the filling
effect above directly, and a measurement of the cost of OpenFHE's noise-flooding
decryption mode.
